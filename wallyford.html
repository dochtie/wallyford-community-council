History of the village

Wallyford sits on high ground looking out over the Firth of Forth, separated from Musselburgh, East Lothian's largest town, by the East Coast railway line. {% include review.html type="check" text="The Welcome leaflet says 115ft above sea level; the 2013 information board says 250 feet." %}

The name is thought to come from the Scots word wally, meaning great or fine. The main road, Salters Road, is named after the monks who used it to carry salt from the pans at Prestonpans to Newbattle Abbey.

For most of its history Wallyford was a mining village, with three pits, a brickworks and the farms around it. Close by are St Clement's Wells, whose distillery was described in 1791 as "the most considerable distillery in Scotland", and Fa'side Castle, a tower house burnt by the English after the Battle of Pinkie, later rebuilt, and saved from demolition and restored in the 1980s.

Wallyford through the years

  • 1600s Coal mining begins locally at the little and muckle Fawside pits.
  • Around 1811 Mining at Dolphingston and Drummore.
  • 1820s to 1857 Wallyford's first pit is sunk in the 1820s, followed by No. 2 pit in 1840 and No. 3 pit in 1857.
  • 4 April 1828 The novelist Margaret Oliphant is born in Wallyford.
  • 1904 The Musselburgh and Fisherrow Co-operative Society opens a Wallyford branch. "The Store" is the heart of village shopping until it closes in the 1970s.
  • 1924 The Edinburgh Coal Company builds the brickworks, making 22,000 bricks a day from red blaes from the pit. The Miners' Institute, built from Wallyford bricks, opens the same year for dances, concerts and snooker.
  • By 1931 Coal production in Wallyford has ended. {% include review.html type="check" text="Some sources say Wallyford Colliery worked until 1947." %}
  • 1972 The brickworks closes.
  • 2010 The Miners' Stone is unveiled in St Clement's Gardens.
  • 2013 The community council commissions the village information board on Salters Road.
  • 2016 onwards Wallyford grows fast, with thousands of new homes planned, a new primary school and the Wallyford Learning Campus. {% include review.html type="check" text="Add opening dates for the new primary school and Learning Campus." %}

Mining and the Miners' Stones

Remembering the men who worked underground

For more than a century, most Wallyford families depended on the pits. The work was hard and dangerous, and 38 men lost their lives in Wallyford's collieries between 1857 and 1929. {% include review.html type="check" text="The Welcome leaflet prints these years as 1957 to 1924; the East Lothian Antiquarians give 1857 to 1929." %}

The Miners' Stone

The Miners' Stone stands in St Clement's Gardens, just off Salters Road at the start of the St Clement's estate. It was commissioned by Wallyford Community Council and unveiled in 2010. Carved into the stone are scenes from underground: shafts, tunnels and the men who worked them. {% include review.html type="check" text="Online sources name the sculptor as Shawn Williamson. Do we want to credit him here?" %}

On either side of the stone are two coal hutches, the wagons that carried coal from the coalface, bearing the names of all 38 miners. Each December, the Torchlight Parade ends here with a short service and a floral tribute to the men.

The carved stones on the Bing

Up on the Bing there are ten more carved stones, each showing a scene from mining life. They're well worth a walk to find. Watch the film of the Bing mining stones.

The Bing

From pit waste to woodland

A bing is the Scots word for a heap of waste from a mine: the shale and stone brought up from the pit along with the coal. Wallyford's bing has been reclaimed as a landscaped park and community woodland, just south-east of the industrial estate.

It's now home to many kinds of wildlife, a network of paths, and the ten carved mining stones. In 2024 East Lothian Council finished a new surfaced and lit path alongside the Bing, so that pupils have a safe route to school all year round.

The Drift

The path to Pinkie

The Drift is the path between Wallyford and Pinkie that passes under the railway line. It's one of the main walking and cycling links between the village and Musselburgh, and part of many people's daily route to the station, the shops and school.

In mining, a drift is a sloping tunnel driven into a coal seam. If you know how the path got its name, we'd love to hear from you.

The Battle of Pinkie

The Battle of Pinkie Cleugh was fought on the fields between Musselburgh, Wallyford and Dalkeith. It was the last major battle between Scotland and England, and the climax of Henry VIII's "Rough Wooing": his attempt to force the Scots to agree to a marriage between his son Edward and the infant Mary, Queen of Scots. Around 10,000 Scots were killed.

A memorial stone stands just off Salters Road. Every year on 10 September, a commemoration is held at the stone at 1pm.

Famous Wallyfordians

Born in Wallyford

Margaret Oliphant (1828 to 1897)

Victorian novelist, essayist, critic, biographer and historian, born in Wallyford on 4 April 1828. J.M. Barrie called her "the most distinguished Scotswoman of her time". The library at the Wallyford Learning Campus is named after her.

Read about her work

Willie Park Senior (1833 to 1903)

Born in Wallyford on 30 June 1833, Willie Park won the very first Open Championship in 1860 and went on to win it four times.

Jock Wallace (1935 to 1996)

The son of a Wallyford miner, Jock Wallace became one of Scottish football's best-known managers, leading Rangers to two domestic trebles in the 1970s.

Remembering

War memorials and remembrance

For the service men and women from Wallyford, Smeaton and Deantown (now Whitecraig) who died in the two world wars and other conflicts.

Remembrance Sunday in Wallyford

  • 10am: service at Wallyford Community Church, Albert Place
  • 10.50am: wreath laying at the war memorial at the foot of St Clement's Crescent
  • 11am: Act of Remembrance

The Crookston War Memorial

Unveiled on 13 September 1919 at Crookston School, which served Wallyford, Smeaton and Deantown, this red sandstone memorial names the local men who fell in the First World War. When the school closed in the late 1950s, the memorial moved to just inside the gates of Inveresk Cemetery in Musselburgh.

In 2014 the community council secured funding from the War Memorials Trust and East Lothian Council to restore it, and held a rededication ceremony on 4 August 2014 to remember all 59 people named on it.

Armistice Day service: 11 November each year, 10.45am.
