A mining village

Wallyford sits on high ground looking out over the Firth of Forth, just across the railway from Musselburgh. The name is thought to come from the Scots word wally, meaning great or fine, and Salters Road is named after the monks who carried salt along it from Prestonpans to Newbattle Abbey.

For most of its history Wallyford was a mining village, with three pits, a brickworks and the farms around it. Since 2016 it has grown faster than ever, with new homes, a new primary school and the Wallyford Learning Campus.

Wallyford through the years
  • 1600s Coal mining begins locally at the little and muckle Fawside pits.
  • 1820s to 1857 Wallyford's first pit is sunk in the 1820s, followed by No. 2 pit in 1840 and No. 3 pit in 1857.
  • 4 April 1828 The novelist Margaret Oliphant is born in Wallyford.
  • 1904 The Co-op opens a Wallyford branch. "The Store" is the heart of village shopping until the 1970s.
  • 1924 The brickworks opens, making 22,000 bricks a day from red blaes from the pit. The Miners' Institute is built from Wallyford bricks the same year.
  • By 1931 Coal production in Wallyford has ended.
  • 1972 The brickworks closes.
  • 2010 The Miners' Stone is unveiled in St Clement's Gardens.
  • 2016 onwards New homes, a new primary school and the Wallyford Learning Campus.

Nearby are St Clement's Wells, whose distillery was described in 1791 as "the most considerable distillery in Scotland", and Fa'side Castle, burnt by the English after the Battle of Pinkie and restored in the 1980s.

Mining and the Miners' Stones

Remembering the men who worked underground

For more than a century, most Wallyford families depended on the pits. The work was hard and dangerous, and 38 men lost their lives in Wallyford's collieries between 1857 and 1929.

The Miners' Stone in St Clement's Gardens, off Salters Road, was commissioned by the Community Council, carved by the sculptor Shawn Williamson, and unveiled in 2010. It is carved with scenes from underground, and the coal hutches on either side carry the names of all 38 miners. Each December the Torchlight Parade ends here with a short service and a floral tribute.

The Bing

From pit waste to woodland

A bing is a heap of waste from a mine. Wallyford's has been turned into a park and community woodland, just south-east of the industrial estate, with paths, wildlife and ten carved stones showing scenes from mining life. A lit path alongside it opened in 2024 as a safe route to school.

The Drift

The path to Pinkie

The Drift is the path between Wallyford and Pinkie that runs under the railway line, one of the main walking and cycling links into Musselburgh. In mining, a drift is a sloping tunnel into a coal seam. If you know how the path got its name, we'd love to hear from you.

The Battle of Pinkie

The Battle of Pinkie Cleugh was fought on the fields between Musselburgh, Wallyford and Dalkeith. It was the last major battle between Scotland and England, part of Henry VIII's "Rough Wooing" to force a marriage between his son Edward and the infant Mary, Queen of Scots. Around 10,000 Scots were killed.

A commemoration is held at the memorial stone off Salters Road every 10 September at 1pm. Watch a short film about the battle.

Famous Wallyfordians

Born in Wallyford

Margaret Oliphant (1828 to 1897)

Victorian novelist, critic and historian. J.M. Barrie called her "the most distinguished Scotswoman of her time". The library at the Learning Campus is named after her. Read about her work.

Willie Park Senior (1833 to 1903)

Won the very first Open Championship in 1860, and three more after it.

Jock Wallace (1935 to 1996)

The son of a Wallyford miner, he led Rangers to two domestic trebles in the 1970s.

Remembering

War memorials and remembrance

For the service men and women from Wallyford, Smeaton and Deantown (now Whitecraig) who died in the two world wars and other conflicts.

Remembrance Sunday in Wallyford

  • 10am: service at Wallyford Community Church, Albert Place
  • 10.50am: wreath laying at the war memorial at the foot of St Clement's Crescent
  • 11am: Act of Remembrance

The Crookston War Memorial

Just inside the gates of Inveresk Cemetery. Armistice Day service: 11 November each year, 10.45am.

The memorial's story

Unveiled on 13 September 1919 at Crookston School, which served Wallyford, Smeaton and Deantown, the red sandstone memorial names the local men who fell in the First World War. It moved to Inveresk Cemetery when the school closed in the late 1950s.

In 2014 the Community Council secured funding from the War Memorials Trust and East Lothian Council to restore it, and held a rededication ceremony on 4 August 2014 for all 59 people named on it. More on War Memorials Online.
